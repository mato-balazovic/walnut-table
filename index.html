The walnut table

I'm making a round dining table from two slabs of American walnut, cut from the same tree. A river of black epoxy joins them. Four hand-shaped legs carry the top.

Top
110 cm round, 4 cm thick
Height

01The woodDone

It started with two slabs

They are a brother and a sister, cut from the same American walnut tree.

That's why their colour and grain match. Both still have their live edges: the natural, wavy sides of the trunk.

Each slab has one flaw I decided to keep. One has a big knot. The other has a hole that goes right through.

Raw walnut looks dusty and grey. Oil brings out the dark chocolate colour and the grain. Drag across the picture to compare.

02The game planDone

More wood, less epoxy

Before cutting anything, I photographed both slabs and laid the top out on the computer.

The two live edges face each other across the middle. Where one curves in, the other curves out, so the river between them stays narrow.

The river is 1.5 cm wide at its narrowest and about 9.5 cm at its widest.

Six ways to place the slabs

I tried six layouts as mock-ups from my photos. The glued ones join the two edges directly, with no river. I chose Diagonal.

03First cutsDone

Cut to length, cleaned to firm wood

On 25 October I cut both slabs to 127 cm, a little longer than the top. The offcuts are set aside for the legs.

On 30 October the bark and the soft layer came off the two facing edges. The wavy shape stays. The knot and the hole are picked clean, and both go right through.

04The pourNext

A river of black epoxy

Next I lay the two pieces out, set the river and build a mould around them.

The black epoxy goes in as one deep pour. It fills the river, the knot and the hole.

Then it has to cure fully before anything else happens.

05Flatten and cutTo come

Flat, sanded, round

Once the epoxy is hard, the top gets flattened on both sides, down to 4 cm.

Small holes that didn't fill get a second round. Then everything is sanded back.

Last, the 110 cm circle is cut.

06The legsTo come

Four legs, one line each

Each leg is a single curve, drawn on the golden ratio. It reaches out under the top, pinches in at the waist and splays out to the floor.

The idea comes from the Spider Table by Foureyes Furniture, which stands on six legs. Mine is adapted to four legs and a 110 cm top.

There's no CNC in this build. A full-size template comes first. Then each leg is cut close to the line and shaped to the template by hand.

The wood comes from the offcuts of the two slabs, plus some extra walnut.

One leg, measured

07The baseTo come

Four legs, one base

The four legs meet in the middle and reach out under the top.

The base gets centred on the underside of the top and fixed to it.

The last step is oil.

08The finished look

How it will look

The table isn't built yet. These pictures are made on the computer, from my photos of the real wood.

09At home

In the corner

The table goes into a corner of my flat, with two cane chairs against the walls.

By day the afternoon sun comes in through the windows. In the evening a lamp hangs over the table.

10The prototype

The table, 10x smaller

On the weekend of 7 and 8 November I made a 1:10 prototype of the table.

It's cut from the offcuts of the two slabs, so it's the same walnut as the real top.

Everything is 10x smaller. The top is 11 cm across and it stands just under 8 cm tall.
